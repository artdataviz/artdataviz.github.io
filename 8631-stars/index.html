Read how it was made ↓

Drag the panorama above and you are looking at every star a dark-adapted eye could see — 8,631 of them, each placed at its measured position and its measured distance, coloured by its measured temperature.

Nothing in it is drawn by hand. But a literal plot of the data would be a black rectangle with a few sub-pixel dots, so a handful of deliberate choices stand between the numbers and the image. This is all of them.

1 · Where the data comes from

One source: the HYG database v4.2 — a merge of the Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star and Gliese catalogues, compiled by Astronexus. 119,626 stars, each with a position, a distance, a brightness and a colour index.

A second, tiny source: Astropy's ephemeris, used once, to find where Earth was on the render date. That is the camera position.

That's it. No image was pasted in, no second catalogue was joined on.

Magnitude 6.5 is the conventional limit of unaided vision under a dark sky. The brightest survivor is Sirius at −1.44; the faintest sit right at 6.49. They span 1.3 to 980 parsecs — 4.3 to 3,198 light years. 389 have proper names; 2,994 more carry a Bayer or Flamsteed designation.

Their positions are used exactly as the catalogue gives them: ICRS equatorial cartesian coordinates in parsecs, epoch J2000. The stars are placed at their true three-dimensional distances inside the scene — not pasted onto the inside of a sphere.

2 · What gets calculated

Three quantities are derived from the catalogue. Everything else is used raw.

Brightness

Apparent magnitude is a logarithmic, backwards scale, so it becomes a linear emission value:

energy = 10 ^ ((6.5 − mag) / 2.5)

Anchored so the faintest visible star is exactly 1.0. Each step of one magnitude is a factor of 2.512. Sirius comes out at 1,500× the faintest star — that ratio is the real dynamic range of the night sky, and it is the central problem of drawing one.

Temperature

The catalogue's B−V colour index becomes a blackbody temperature via Ballesteros' 2012 fit:

T = 4600 · ( 1/(0.92·ci + 1.7) + 1/(0.92·ci + 0.62) )

The 8,631 stars span 2,213 K (deep red giants) to 16,991 K (blue-white O and B stars). 31 stars have no colour index recorded and default to solar white.

Colour

Temperature becomes linear RGB through Tanner Helland's blackbody approximation, normalised so the brightest channel is 1.0 — the triple carries hue, and brightness is left to energy alone.

3 · What was adjusted to make it visible

This is the honest part of any data visualisation: the places where the image departs from the data on purpose.

Every star is drawn the same size

Real stars are point sources — all of them, without exception. So a rendered star needs some invented size, and the obvious choice is wrong.

The stars sit at their true distances, from 1.3 to 980 parsecs. Give them all the same physical radius and the nearest would appear 740× larger than the farthest, which is a picture of distance, not of brightness. Instead each star's radius scales with its distance:

radius = 1.10e-3 radians × distance

so every star subtends the same angle — 5.63 pixels across, at this panorama's 80.6 arcseconds per pixel. The distance terms then cancel exactly, and how bright a star looks depends only on its magnitude. Which is precisely what apparent magnitude means.

The exposure is calibrated, not eyeballed

Emission is scaled so that a magnitude 6.5 star lands around 57/255 — faint but unmistakably present — and Sirius saturates. That single constant was set by measuring a crop of the full-resolution render, not by turning a dial until it looked nice.

Bloom does the work that pixels can't

A star 1,500× brighter than another cannot be shown by pixel value alone; 8-bit pixels run out after 255. So the render adds a Fog Glow bloom, which spreads the excess brightness outward instead of clipping it — the same thing your eye and any lens do. It is why the bright stars read as bright rather than merely white. AgX tone mapping then compresses the range into 8 bits without flattening the cores.

Everything else — position, distance, relative brightness, colour temperature — is the catalogue's, untouched.

4 · How it's framed

The projection is equirectangular, 2:1: right ascension runs horizontally with 0h at the centre, declination runs linearly up and down, and the north celestial pole is stretched along the top edge.

Right ascension increases leftward, which looks wrong until you think about where you are standing. You are inside the celestial sphere looking out. Making RA run rightward would require a mirrored transform — a sky reflected east-for-west. Eleven unit tests check this against known star positions, because it is exactly the kind of error that looks fine until an astronomer sees it.

The viewpoint is the centre of the Earth on 2000-01-01, taken from the ephemeris. That is 4.85e-6 parsecs from the Sun, which buys a parallax of at most 0.77 arcseconds against the nearest star — about 0.005 of a pixel. Correct, and completely invisible. Some things are worth doing properly anyway.

One artefact worth naming: stars very near the poles get smeared horizontally by the projection. Polaris, at declination +89.26°, is stretched 78× along the top edge. That is the projection being correct, not the render being broken — the viewer maps it back onto a sphere, where Polaris is round again.

5 · How it's rendered

Each star is a tiny emissive sphere in Blender, rendered with Cycles at 512 samples through an equirectangular panoramic camera, at 16,084 × 8,042. Denoising is switched off: emissive spheres on a black background have nothing to denoise, and it costs two minutes and 41 GB of memory to achieve nothing.

krpano then slices the equirectangular image into cube-face tiles at five resolutions, which is what lets the viewer above load instantly and stay sharp as you zoom.

6 · What this is not

  • No Milky Way. The band you see in a real dark sky is unresolved starlight — stars far too faint for this catalogue. 8,631 stars cannot make it, so it isn't there.
  • No atmosphere, no horizon. This is the celestial sphere, not the view from a particular field at a particular hour.
  • No proper motion. Every position is frozen at epoch J2000.
  • No interstellar dust. Real starlight is dimmed and reddened on its way here; none of that is modelled.
  • Variable stars get one brightness, the catalogue's, not tonight's.
  • Stars only. No planets, no Moon, no nebulae, no galaxies.
