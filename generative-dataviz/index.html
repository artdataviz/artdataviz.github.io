Dataviz · 288 page loads

One website behind two CDNs, timed from four cities in May 2024. Every bar is a column of ones and zeros: the bits move, the heights don't.

Read how it was made ↓

I found this chart while cleaning my laptop: a generative-dataviz experiment from May 2024. It compares one website served two ways, through Amazon CloudFront and through Cloudflare, each on its default settings.

This page is about the chart: why the bars are made of bits, how they move, and how the colours were measured. The experiment behind it gets one section.

1 · The data, at a glance

A client's virtual-tour site, deployed twice: one copy behind Amazon CloudFront, one behind Cloudflare, both on default settings. Both delivered the same page: a median 3.34 MB in 78 requests.

On 16 May 2024, WebPageTest loaded 4 tours from 4 cities through both CDNs, 9 times each: 288 page loads in desktop Chrome, on a 5 Mbps cable profile, with an empty cache every time.

2 · Why the bars are made of bits

Height carries the value, as in any bar chart. Colour carries the CDN. The bits carry no data, but they are not random decoration: the test measured how long a page's bits take to cross the internet, so the bars are built from bits.

As texture, they make the bars breathe. Bits stream down, flash and fade like data in transit, while the heights hold still.

Generative, within rules. Chance draws the bits, and four rules limit it:

  • Five characters per row, each a 1, a 0 or a space.
  • The top row is always solid. It never gets a space, so each bar's exact height reads at a glance.
  • Below it, spaces fall by probability: 4 in 25 on the bottom row, 12 in 35 in the body. The base stays denser than the body.
  • Opacity is random too. 70% of bits sit at full opacity, the rest at 57–100%.

Every render draws new bits, and the heights never move; the published loop fixes seed 2024.

One row = 77 ms. The number is an artistic choice. At 77 ms per row, every row of bits stays visible, and the glyphs are big enough to read and small enough to keep precision. Rows round down, so a bar can sit up to 76 ms short of its median. The smallest gap in the chart is 396 ms, so rounding never flips a comparison.

3 · How it moves

The heights stay fixed, so every frame is a correct chart. The bits stream down inside the bars.

A looping tape per column of bits. Row r at frame f shows tape cell r + f // speed. One step later, every cell sits one row lower.

r = np.arange(n_rows - 1)[:, None]               # body rows, bottom first
idx = (r + f // speeds) % (frames // speeds)     # one row down per speed step

Three speeds. Each column steps one row every 2, 4 or 6 frames, picked at random. 192 frames divide by all three, so the last frame runs into the first: an 8.6 s loop at 22.4 fps.

The top row stays put. Its bits flip twice per loop, and it never blanks or fades, so the edge that carries the value holds still.

Sparkle. At any frame, 5.6% of bits are mid-flash. A flash lasts 0.22 s, lifts opacity to 100% and pales the colour 60% toward white. It rides down with its bit.

Dissolve. At any frame, 5% of bits are mid-fade. A fade lasts 1.4 s and bottoms out at 10% of the bit's opacity.

39 tests hold the rules in place. Among them: bar height is the same in every frame, the top row never blanks, and sparkle and dissolve hit their rates.

4 · Colour, measured

The hues come from the two logos: Cloudflare orange and CloudFront purple. The lightness comes from a measurement.

In 2024 I picked #E5873B and #895FEE by eye. Measured in OKLCH, they sat at lightness 0.71 and 0.60. The purple was darker, so CloudFront, the faster CDN, looked dimmer as well as shorter: the colour argued against the data.

The fix kept the purple's hue, 293°, and lifted its lightness to 0.71: #A68BFE. Now neither CDN reads louder, and only height differs.

Why OKLCH, not HSL. OKLCH is built so that equal L looks equally light. HSL is not: it rates the orange at 57% and the new purple at 77%, while OKLCH rates both 0.71.

Threshold lines follow the same rule. Google grades LCP in three bands: good up to 2,500 ms, needs improvement up to 4,000 ms, poor above. The chart draws both borders.

Until this version, lime #ADE63C and red #E64D3C at 80% opacity measured L 0.75 and 0.56 on the background: the “good” line shone brighter than the bars, and the two lines didn't match. Now both sit at L 0.64 and chroma 0.165: equal to each other, and a step behind the bars. Reference lines frame the data; they shouldn't outshine it.

How to do it in production

  • CSS takes OKLCH directly in every current browser: color: oklch(0.71 0.164 292.5). Give every series the same L.
  • Python: coloraide converts, adjusts and fits the result back into sRGB.
  • By eye: oklch.com shows which chroma still fits the screen gamut at a given lightness.
  • Check colour blindness after. About 1 in 12 men sees colour differently, most often mixing up reds and greens. Matched lightness takes away the brightness cue, so hue alone has to keep the bars apart. A simulator shows the pair as those eyes see it, and ΔE measures how far apart the two colours look: 0 is identical, 8 is the usual minimum for chart colours. Orange and purple score 27 for red-blind and green-blind vision: still easy to tell apart.
from coloraide import Color                        # pip install coloraide

purple = Color('#895FEE').convert('oklch')         # L 0.60, C 0.205, h 292.7
purple['lightness'] = 0.71                          # match the orange
purple.convert('srgb').fit().to_string(hex=True)    # '#a68aff', one step off #A68BFE

5 · Layout

  • Labels turn a number into a judgement. A line alone says 2,500. The label says what it means: “good” with ↓, since good loads fall below the line, and “poor” with ↑. The words carry the meaning, so red against green is never the only cue.
  • Bars cut the lines. A background panel behind each bar breaks a line where a bar stands. Dots never cross the digits, and every bar keeps a clean edge.
  • Order sets the direction. Cities are sorted by the Cloudflare median, 3.0 s to 4.9 s. The orange bars climb left to right, the purple ones stay under 3 s, and the gap widens at every step: +396, +1,264, +1,538, +2,097 ms. The chart reads as one movement, not four separate comparisons.
  • The subtitle is the legend. “● Cloudflare vs ● Amazon CloudFront” names the comparison and keys the colours in one line. No legend box sits in the plot, so the space stays with the data.
  • Text stays neutral. Labels are white at 70%, names in title ink. Only the dots and the bits wear the CDN colours.

6 · Make your own

The generator takes a CSV of category, group and value, and writes a PNG, an MP4 and a GIF. It needs Python 3.10+ and ffmpeg.

python binary_bars.py lcp_medians.csv --out chart --row-value 77 \
    --hline '2500:#719D00:good:down' --hline '4000:#DE5D4D:poor:up'
