Dataviz · Every bit counts

One website behind two CDNs, timed from four cities in May 2024. Every bar is a column of ones and zeros: the bits move, the heights don't.

Read how it was made ↓

I found this chart while cleaning up my laptop. It's a generative-dataviz experiment from May 2024: one website served two ways, through Amazon CloudFront and through Cloudflare, both left on default settings.

This page is mostly about how the chart is made: why the bars are built from bits, how they move, and how I measured the colours. The experiment itself gets one short section.

1 · The data, briefly

The site is a client's virtual tour, deployed twice, once behind each CDN. Both copies serve the same page, a median 3.34 MB in 78 requests.

On 16 May 2024 I ran it through WebPageTest from London, Dubai, Singapore and Cape Town: four tours, both CDNs, nine runs each. That's 288 page loads in desktop Chrome on a 5 Mbps cable profile, every one of them with an empty cache.

  2. Pythonrequests, pandas
  3. 32 CSVs288 loads

Each bar is the median Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) for one city and one CDN, the moment the largest element on the page appears. It's one setup, on one day, in a lab, so please don't read it as a verdict on either CDN.

2 · Why bits

As in any bar chart, height carries the value and colour tells the two CDNs apart. The ones and zeros don't encode anything extra.

They aren't random decoration either. The whole test is about how long a page's bits take to travel across the internet, so building the bars out of bits felt right. They also make the bars feel alive. Bits drip down, flash and fade, and the heights stay exactly where the data puts them.

The bits are generated, with a few rules to keep chance in check. Each row has five characters, and each one is a 1, a 0 or a space. The top row never gets a space, so you can see exactly where a bar ends. Below it, spaces show up by probability: 4 in 25 on the bottom row and 12 in 35 in the body, which keeps the base denser. Opacity is random too. 70% of the bits are fully opaque, and the rest land somewhere between 57% and 100%.

Every render draws a new set of bits, and the heights never change. The version on this page uses seed 2024.

One row stands for 77 ms. It's an artistic choice, after all. At 77 ms every row is still visible, and the characters are big enough to read but small enough to keep the chart precise. Rows round down, so a bar can come up to 76 ms short. The smallest gap between two bars is 396 ms, so the rounding never changes which one is taller.

3 · How it moves

The heights are fixed, so every single frame is a correct chart. Only the bits move.

Each column of bits reads from its own looping tape. At frame f, row r shows tape cell r + f // speed, so with every step the whole column slides down one row.

r = np.arange(n_rows - 1)[:, None]               # body rows, bottom first
idx = (r + f // speeds) % (frames // speeds)     # one row down per speed step

Columns move at one of three speeds: one row every 2, 4 or 6 frames. The loop is 192 frames long, and all three speeds divide into it, so the last frame flows straight into the first. At 22.4 fps that's 8.6 seconds.

The top row stays put. Its bits flip twice per loop, but it never blanks or fades, because that edge is the value.

Two effects sit on top. At any moment 5.6% of the bits are flashing: for 0.22 s they turn fully opaque and 60% paler, and ride down with their column. Another 5% are dissolving, fading over 1.4 s to a tenth of their opacity.

39 tests keep all of this honest. For example, bar height is identical in every frame, and the top row never has a gap.

4 · Measuring colour

This is my favourite part. The hues come from the two logos, Cloudflare orange and CloudFront purple. The lightness, though, I measured.

Back in 2024 I picked #E5873B and #895FEE by eye. When I measured them in OKLCH this year, the orange came out at lightness 0.71 and the purple at 0.60. So the purple bars were darker, and CloudFront, the faster CDN, looked dimmer as well as shorter. The colour was arguing against the data.

The fix was to keep the purple's hue, 293°, and lift its lightness to 0.71. That gives #A68BFE. Now neither CDN looks louder than the other, and height is the only difference.

Why OKLCH and not HSL? OKLCH is designed so that the same L looks equally light to the eye. HSL isn't. It calls the orange 57% light and the new purple 77%, while OKLCH puts both at 0.71.

The threshold lines got the same treatment. Google grades LCP in three bands: good up to 2,500 ms, needs improvement up to 4,000 ms, and poor above that. The chart draws both borders.

In the previous version the lines were lime #ADE63C and red #E64D3C at 80% opacity. Measured against the background, that's L 0.75 and 0.56. The “good” line was brighter than the bars themselves, and the two lines didn't match each other. Now both sit at L 0.64 with chroma 0.165, equal to each other and a notch below the bars. Reference lines are there to frame the data, and they shouldn't shout louder than it.

Doing this in production

In CSS you can write OKLCH directly, and every current browser supports it: color: oklch(0.71 0.164 292.5). Give all your series the same L and you're most of the way there.

In Python, coloraide does the conversion and pulls the result back into the sRGB gamut:

from coloraide import Color                        # pip install coloraide

purple = Color('#895FEE').convert('oklch')         # L 0.60, C 0.205, h 292.7
purple['lightness'] = 0.71                          # match the orange
purple.convert('srgb').fit().to_string(hex=True)    # '#a68aff', one step off #A68BFE

If you'd rather pick by eye, oklch.com shows which chroma values still fit on screen at a given lightness.

Then check colour blindness. About 1 in 12 men sees colour differently, most often mixing up reds and greens. Once lightness is matched, brightness no longer helps tell the bars apart, so hue has to do all the work. A simulator shows the pair the way those eyes see it, and ΔE measures how far apart the two colours look: 0 means identical, and 8 is the usual minimum for chart colours. Orange and purple score 27 for red-blind and green-blind vision, so they're still easy to tell apart.

5 · Small layout decisions

A line on its own only says “2,500”. The labels say what that means: “good” with an arrow pointing down, since good loads fall below the line, and “poor” with an arrow pointing up. The words carry the meaning, so nobody has to rely on telling red from green.

Bars cut through the lines. Each bar has a panel of background colour behind it, one layer above the lines, so the dots stop where a bar starts and never run across the digits.

The cities are sorted from the smallest orange bar to the biggest, so the bars climb in a diagonal across the chart. That's a composition choice more than a data one. A diagonal gives the eye a direction, left to right. The purple bars stay low, so the gap between the two grows along the way, and you read the chart as one movement instead of four separate comparisons.

There's no legend box. The subtitle does that job: “● Cloudflare vs ● Amazon CloudFront”, each dot in its bar colour. It names the comparison and explains the colours in one line, and the plot stays free for the data.

All the text stays neutral: white at 70% for the labels, title ink for the names. Only the dots and the bits carry the CDN colours.

6 · Make your own

The generator is on GitHub. It takes one CSV and writes a PNG, an MP4 and a GIF. You'll need Python 3.10+ and ffmpeg.

The CSV has three columns and one row per bar, in the order you want the bars drawn. This is the file behind the chart above:

category,group,value
London,Cloudflare,3049.0
London,Amazon CloudFront,2653.0
Dubai,Cloudflare,3387.0
Dubai,Amazon CloudFront,2123.0
Singapore,Cloudflare,4449.0
Singapore,Amazon CloudFront,2911.5
Cape Town,Cloudflare,4915.0
Cape Town,Amazon CloudFront,2818.0
  • category is the label under each cluster of bars, here the city.
  • group is the bar inside a cluster, here the CDN. Each group gets its own colour, in the order it first appears. Two groups work out of the box; for more, pass --colors.
  • value is the bar's height in your own units, here milliseconds.

Then run:

python binary_bars.py lcp_medians.csv --out chart --row-value 77 \
    --hline '2500:#719D00:good:down' --hline '4000:#DE5D4D:poor:up'

--row-value sets how many units one row of bits stands for; leave it out and the tallest bar gets 63 rows. --hline draws a reference line from a value, a colour, and optionally a label and an arrow direction. Repeat it for more lines.

--glyphs swaps the ones and zeros for any two symbols. Bits suit this chart because bits are what the test measured, and other data can have symbols of its own: zZ for sleep, $+ for sales, +- for code changes. The space stays for the gaps.
