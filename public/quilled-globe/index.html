Where people live, in paper. Every coil on this globe is wound from a strip whose length follows the population under it. Drag to turn it; pinch, or click and scroll, to come closer.

Click the globe, then scroll to zoom · or pinch

A one-metre globe covered in 66,441 strips of 5 mm paper, stood on edge and rolled into coils the way quilling is done by hand. The more people live under a coil, the longer its strip, so a crowded place is wound tight and red and an empty one loose and pale. It is a tight red coil because there is literally more paper in it.

1 · The data

Population comes from the European Commission's GHS-POP grid for 2020: people counted in one-kilometre cells over the whole planet, on an equal-area projection so that summing cells under a coil needs no correction. Borders come from Natural Earth. Every country is outlined by a wall strip, 753 in all, and filled with coils: 38,089 on land. The sea is filled too, with 27,599 blue coils that carry no data: small along every coast and growing toward open water, the way a quiller fills around a shape.

2 · The paper

Each coil stands on 1,100 to 18,000 km² of ground, and its strip length follows the people living there; the ring spacing runs from 2.5 mm for a loose coil to 0.35 mm, near solid, against 0.2 mm paper. Colour repeats the same number on a ramp from manila to crimson. Each coil is set down by hand, as it were: a hair taller or shorter, leaning up to a degree, from a slightly different dye lot.

3 · What you are seeing

The globe was built as geometry, 17 million points of paper, and rendered in Blender. What loads here is that render baked into 640 texture tiles: colour, the angle of the paper, and how much light reaches between the turns. Your browser lights them again, with the same three studio lights the renders used and a warm sun you can dial in. Tiles load as you look at them, sharper ones as you come closer.

Close up, the coils are still a picture of paper. Real paper geometry, streamed in as you zoom, is the next version.
